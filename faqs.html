TMV basics

What TMVs do, and why London ones need extra care

What is a TMV?

A TMV (thermostatic mixing valve) blends hot and cold water to a safe, steady temperature before it reaches a tap, shower or bath. Inside is a thermostat that keeps adjusting the mix as supply temperatures and pressures change. If the cold supply fails, a working valve shuts down or slows the flow to a trickle so nobody is hit with hot water.

Why do TMVs in London need servicing more often than some areas?

Mostly because of hard water. Much of London is supplied with hard water, and limescale builds up on the thermostat, the seats and the inlet strainers. A scaled-up valve responds slowly, drifts off its setting and may not shut off properly when the cold fails. You can check the hardness for your postcode on Thames Water's water quality checker. Our hard water and limescale article goes into more detail.

TMV2 or TMV3: which do London buildings usually have?

Flats, HMOs and most homes use TMV2 valves, which are for domestic-type installations and tested to BS EN 1111 or BS EN 1287. Hospitals, clinics, care homes and many schools use TMV3 valves, which are certified against the NHS performance specification D 08. Older blocks often have a mix of both, plus a few unmarked valves nobody can identify. We note the type of every valve on your asset register.

We have thermostatic taps. Do we also need a TMV?

Usually not, and you shouldn't have both feeding the same outlet. HTM 04-01 Part B (paragraph 6.40) says TMVs should not be installed in series with mixing taps. It also asks for pipework to existing thermostatic taps to be checked, so that no TMV feeds the tap's hot inlet (paragraph 7.42). Doubling up gives you a run of lukewarm pipe and two devices to maintain instead of one. We flag any we find.

Can a TMV be fitted under a basin, behind a panel or in the ceiling?

Yes. Valves are fitted in all those places, but each one must be reachable for testing and servicing, and the mixed-water pipe after it should be kept as short as possible. In older London conversions we often find valves boxed in behind tiled panels with no access hatch. If that's your building, we'll tell you which ones need an access panel adding before they can be serviced properly.

Rules and guidance

HTM 04-01, D 08, ACOP L8 and the rest

Do London landlords have to fit and service TMVs?

No single law names TMVs for every building. The duty comes from managing the risks you control. Employers and duty holders do that under the Health and Safety at Work etc. Act 1974. For rented homes, scalding from hot water is one of the hazards a council can assess under the Housing Health and Safety Rating System. In new homes, Approved Document G (G3) limits hot water to a bath to 48°C. Where a scalding risk assessment says you need a TMV, it has to be kept working, which means servicing.

Will a London council ask about TMVs when licensing an HMO?

Not usually by name. Licence conditions vary between boroughs, but they don't normally list TMVs. An officer inspecting the property can still look at hot water safety as part of their hazard assessment, especially where tenants are vulnerable. Having valves serviced and records ready makes that conversation easier. Our HMO licensing article covers this in more depth.

What does HTM 04-01 ask for when it comes to TMVs?

HTM 04-01 is the NHS guidance on safe water in healthcare buildings. Part B (2016) says a scalding risk assessment decides where TMVs are needed (6.35), and that they should only be fitted where it shows a need (7.40). Their settings and operation must be checked regularly, following Chapter 11 of the D 08 supplement (6.36 and 7.41). Its maintenance checklist covers cleaning, descaling and disinfecting TMV strainers annually, or as the risk assessment directs.

We run a private clinic in London. Does HTM 04-01 apply to us?

HTM 04-01 is written for NHS premises, but private hospitals, clinics, dental practices and care providers widely use it as the benchmark for good practice. If you're registered with the CQC, inspectors will expect you to show that your premises and equipment are safe, and HTM 04-01 is the obvious yardstick for water. We service TMV3 valves in clinics to the D 08 procedure. See our article on HTM 04-01 in London clinics.

What is D 08?

D 08 is the NHS performance specification for thermostatic mixing valves in healthcare premises. It's published as a supplement to HTM 04-01 (2017 edition). Most of it is laboratory type-testing for manufacturers. Chapter 11 is the part that matters on site, setting out the commissioning and in-service tests. Annex F suggests how to work out how often to test.

What maximum temperatures does D 08 set?

Table 17 of D 08 gives maximum mixed-water settings by outlet. They are 38°C for a bidet, 41°C for a shower, and 41°C for a washbasin where people wash under running water. Baths are 44°C, or 46°C where the bather is always supervised by a competent person such as a nurse or carer. A lower setting can be chosen after a risk assessment. HSE's HSIS6 says water for whole-body bathing should not exceed 44°C where vulnerable people are at risk.

Where do ACOP L8 and HSG274 fit in?

They cover Legionella rather than scalding, but TMVs sit right where the two meet. ACOP L8 requires a Legionella risk assessment and a written control scheme. HSG274 Part 2 gives the technical detail for hot and cold water systems, including how TMVs should be maintained. Your TMV service records belong in the same logbook as the rest of your water safety checks.

Testing and servicing

How often, what's involved and what counts as a fail

How often should TMVs be tested?

For new valves, Annex F of D 08 suggests tests at 6 to 8 weeks and 12 to 15 weeks after commissioning. After that, the gap depends on how far each valve drifted. Little or no change (about 1°C) means the next test can wait until 24 to 28 weeks. Bigger changes bring it forward to 18 to 21 weeks. From then on, you use the longest interval that past tests show the valve can hold. HTM 04-01 Part B adds that local water quality affects how often valves need maintenance, which is very relevant in hard-water London.

What actually happens when you test a TMV?

We run the outlet at full flow and record the mixed temperature. Then we isolate the cold supply to check that the valve shuts off. After restoring the cold, we check the temperature settles back where it should be. On a service visit we also clean or descale the strainers, check valves and thermostat as needed, then reset and retest the valve. See TMV servicing for the full visit.

What counts as a pass on the fail-safe (cold isolation) test?

Under clause 11.2 of D 08, the cold supply is isolated and the outlet watched. If water is still flowing after 5 seconds, it's collected for 60 seconds. More than 120 ml means the valve needs service work or recommissioning. Once the cold is restored, the temperature after 15 seconds should be within 2°C of the earlier reading. If it isn't, check the supplies and recommission the valve.

Do the hot and cold supplies matter?

Yes, a lot. Table 18 of D 08 gives the conditions a TMV3 valve expects in normal use: hot water of at least 55°C and cold of no more than 20°C. It also sets pressure ranges for high and low pressure systems. London's cold mains can warm up in a long summer, especially in pipes that run through warm ceiling voids in flats. If we find the supplies out of range, we record it and tell you, because a valve can't make up for supplies that are wrong.

Do the strainers really need cleaning every year?

The maintenance checklist in HTM 04-01 Part B lists inspecting, cleaning, descaling and disinfecting TMV strainers annually, or at a frequency set by the risk assessment, taking account of the manufacturer's advice. In London we often find strainers part-blocked with scale and debris well before a year is up. A blocked strainer starves one side of the valve and throws the temperature off.

Can you service TMVs without shutting off water to the whole building?

Normally, yes. Most valves have isolating valves on their inlets, so we can work on one outlet at a time while everyone else carries on. Where there aren't any, we'll explain the options and plan any shutdown with you in advance. In occupied flats, care homes and hotels we work room by room, so disruption stays small.

A TMV failed. Should we repair it or replace it?

If the body is sound and parts are available, a service kit (thermostat, seals, check valves) usually sorts it out. Replacement makes sense when parts are discontinued, the body is corroded, the valve keeps failing or it's the wrong type for the outlet, such as a TMV2 in a care setting. We'll show you the readings and give you a price for both options before doing anything. See installation and replacement.

Legionella and quiet outlets

Where scalding and Legionella control meet

Can a TMV make Legionella more likely?

It can if it's badly placed or rarely used. Water leaving a TMV sits in the temperature range where bacteria grow best. HTM 04-01 Part B (4.3) points out that pipework downstream of TMVs can be a particular problem. That's why the mixed-water pipe should be short, the outlet used regularly, and the valve cleaned and disinfected when it's serviced. HSE's Legionella pages explain the wider picture.

What about outlets that are hardly used?

Flush them. HSG274 Part 2 generally recommends flushing infrequently used outlets at least once a week, and HTM 04-01 Part B notes healthcare sites may need to do it more often. In London that often means empty flats between lets, spare guest rooms in hotels, and schools over the holidays. Keep a log of the flushing, because an untested record is as good as no record.

Should we take out TMVs we don't need?

Possibly. HTM 04-01 Part B says thermostatic mixing devices should only be installed where a risk assessment shows a need. A valve with no scalding risk to manage still adds a warm dead leg and a maintenance job. Removing one is a decision for your scalding and Legionella risk assessments, not something to do on a whim. We'll point out candidates and you can discuss them with your Responsible Person.

Our building has been empty for weeks. What should happen to the TMVs?

Before people move back in, the outlets should be flushed and the TMVs checked. Valves that have sat still can stick, and strainers can collect debris when the system is drained or disturbed. Follow your written scheme. It should say whether disinfection or sampling is needed after a long shutdown. Our article on schools over the summer holidays covers a typical case.
