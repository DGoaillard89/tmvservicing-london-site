The first proper cold snap of the winter tends to arrive with a bit of chaos. A main gives up under a road in Fulham, a pipe freezes in a loft in Lewisham, or the water company turns off a street for an emergency repair. The water comes back a few hours later, everyone breathes out, and then the showers start doing odd things.

Sound familiar? Here's what an interrupted supply does to thermostatic mixing valves, and what we'd check before anyone steps into a bath.

Why an outage upsets a TMV

A TMV is a balancing act. It takes hot and cold, and its thermostatic element constantly adjusts the mix to hold a set temperature. It needs both supplies to arrive at a sensible pressure and a steady temperature. Cut one of them off and bring it back with a bang, and you've thrown the whole thing a curveball.

A few things usually happen when the water goes off and comes back:

  • Debris gets shaken loose. When a main is repaired and refilled, the rush of water stirs up sediment and scale that's been sitting quietly in the pipes for years. London's hard water means there's usually plenty of it. It travels to the first fine mesh it meets, which is often a TMV strainer.
  • Air gets in. Air pockets cause spluttering outlets, noisy pipes and surges in pressure. A TMV will chase those surges and the outlet temperature can swing about until the air's cleared.
  • One side comes back before the other. In plenty of London buildings the cold comes straight off the main while the hot comes from a cylinder, a tank-fed system or communal plant. They don't always recover at the same speed.

Frozen pipes are a special case

A frozen cold feed is basically a fail-safe test nobody planned. A healthy TMV should shut off or drop to a trickle when its cold supply disappears, which is exactly what you want. A tired valve with a sluggish element might not, and that's when you get very hot water at the outlet.

The other worry is the valve itself. Water expands as it freezes, and a TMV that's frozen solid in an unheated void, an outbuilding or a pipe boxing on an outside wall can end up with damaged internals or a cracked body. It might look fine and still not work properly. We'd always get a valve that's been frozen tested before it goes back into normal use.

The usual suspects in London are loft spaces in Victorian and Edwardian conversions, roof-level tank rooms on mansion blocks and low-rise council blocks, and the odd changing room or toilet block in a school or park that sits empty in the cold. If you've got TMVs anywhere like that, it's worth checking the lagging before the next frost.

Please don't turn the set point up or down to "fix" it. A valve that's misbehaving after an outage usually has a blocked strainer or air in the system, not a wrong setting. Adjust it now and you'll be chasing the wrong temperature once things settle.

What to do when the water comes back on

Nothing complicated, but the order matters.

  1. Follow the water company's advice first. If the water arrives cloudy or discoloured, they'll usually tell you to run the cold kitchen tap until it clears. Do that before anything else.
  2. Let the hot water recover. If the boiler or plant went off too, give it time to get stored water back up to temperature. HSG274 Part 2 expects stored hot water at 60°C or above, and a TMV can't deliver a stable mix from a half-warm cylinder.
  3. Run each outlet for a while. Clear the air and any loose debris. If something has sat still for longer than usual, follow the flushing steps in your written scheme, because standing water is a Legionella concern under ACOP L8.
  4. Take a temperature at TMV outlets. A simple thermometer reading at the outlet tells you a lot. If it's stable and around where it normally sits, good. If it's swinging about, low on flow or noticeably off, leave it and get it looked at.
  5. Prioritise the vulnerable. In a care home, a supported living scheme or anywhere with young children, check every TMV outlet before baths and showers are used again. That's the place to be cautious.
  6. Write it down. Note when the water went off, when it came back and what you found. It's useful for your Legionella records and it helps us if we're called out.

What we'd do on a call-out like this

Imagine a 1930s mansion block in Hammersmith and Fulham after a burst main on the street outside. The water's back, but residents on a couple of floors say their baths keep going hot and cold.

On a visit like that, we'd usually start by checking the hot and cold supply temperatures and pressures at the affected flats. Then we'd isolate a valve, pull its strainers and see what's in them. After a burst, it's often grit, flakes of scale and the odd bit of rust. Clean strainers, check the check valves, then do a proper temperature and fail-safe test on each valve against its recorded set point. If a valve's element has taken a knock, it gets a cartridge or a replacement, not a new setting.

We'd leave the managing agent with the readings for each valve and a note of anything upstream that needs a look, like a booster pump that's struggling or a cylinder that isn't recovering.

A quick pre-winter checklist

  • Lag pipes and TMVs in lofts, voids and unheated rooms.
  • Know where your stopcocks and TMV isolation valves are, and label them.
  • Keep a basic thermometer somewhere sensible so whoever's on site can take a reading.
  • Check your service records. Valves already flagged as scaled or slow are the ones most likely to sulk after an outage.

The short version

When the water's been off, assume the TMVs have had a rough morning. Let the system settle, clear the air and debris, take some temperatures and get anything odd checked properly.
