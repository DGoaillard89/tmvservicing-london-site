Servicing a TMV is a job of under an hour. Getting to it in London can take longer, if nobody's planned. Every engineer working in this city knows the feeling: you've found the building, there's nowhere to stop, the loading bay is full of a delivery lorry and the concierge has never heard of you. Autumn is a good time to sort this out, with the schools back, the roads busy again and the autumn servicing round under way.

Why it matters to you, not just us

A wasted visit is a cost to everyone. If we can't park, can't get in or can't reach the valves, the work doesn't get done and the return visit has to be booked. On a big building, a bad start to the morning can knock out a whole floor's worth of servicing. A bit of planning beforehand makes the day cheaper, quicker and much less stressful for your residents or staff.

Parking: the London obstacle course

Controlled parking zones

Most of inner London, and a lot of outer London, is covered by CPZs. Hours and rules differ from borough to borough and even zone to zone. Some only apply for a couple of hours in the middle of the day; others run late into the evening and at weekends. We check the restrictions for every address before the day.

If your building can provide a visitor permit or a virtual permit through the council's system, that helps a lot. Some councils also let you book a parking bay suspension in advance for contractors, but the notice periods and fees vary, so if it's needed, it's worth starting early.

Red routes and loading bays

Red routes are run by Transport for London and have strict stopping rules. Loading bays have their own time limits and are often shared with every shop on the parade. For buildings on main roads, a booked slot in a private loading bay, service yard or basement car park is far better than hoping for the best.

Congestion Charge and ULEZ

Central London jobs fall inside the Congestion Charge zone, and the Ultra Low Emission Zone now covers all of Greater London. These are part of the cost of working here, and they're one of the reasons we group central London visits together rather than dotting them across the week.

Tell us the honest truth about parking. "There's usually a space round the back" is great if it's true. If it's only true on Sunday mornings, we'd rather know.

Getting into the building

Parking sorted, the next hurdle is the front door. What tends to help:

  • A named contact on the day. A caretaker, porter, concierge or site manager who knows we're coming.
  • Keys or fobs arranged in advance, especially for plant rooms, riser cupboards and communal areas.
  • Sign-in and permits sorted. Offices, hospitals and some residential blocks need induction, permits to work or a security pass. Tell us what's needed and we'll send details ahead.
  • Goods lift access in tall buildings. Carrying kit up eleven floors builds character but doesn't speed things up.

Getting into homes and rooms

Most TMVs are inside somebody's flat, room, ward or classroom. In residential buildings, this is usually where the day succeeds or fails. The approach that works best is boringly simple:

  1. Send notice letters to residents, ideally a week or two ahead, with the date, a time window and a number to call to rearrange. We can draft these for you.
  2. Remind people the day before, through a noticeboard, a text or the residents' WhatsApp group if there is one.
  3. Agree what happens with no-access flats. Do you hold keys? Can a member of staff let us in? Or do we book a return visit?
  4. Track the gaps. We keep a list of every valve we couldn't reach and why, so the return visit is planned rather than forgotten.

In hotels the same thinking applies with housekeeping instead of letters. We've written about that in servicing TMVs in London hotels.

Inside the flat: a clear run at the panel

Bath panels, boxed-in pipework and vanity units tend to collect things: cleaning products, spare towels, the occasional bicycle. A note in the resident letter asking for clear access to the bath panel and under the basin saves a surprising amount of time. If a valve is tiled in with no access at all, we'll record it and suggest a neat access panel, rather than taking a chisel to someone's bathroom on the spot. Our installation page covers access panels and replacements.

A typical scenario

Imagine a 40-flat mansion block in Hammersmith in a CPZ that runs most of the day, with no on-site parking and a lift that just about fits two people. A well-planned day might look like this: a visitor permit sorted by the managing agent, resident letters sent ten days before, a porter with a set of keys for agreed flats, and a route through the building floor by floor from the top down. Flats with no access are logged, and a second visit is booked for a Saturday morning for residents who work weekdays. Nothing clever, just sorted ahead of time.

Planning across London

We're based in Chiswick, so West London is home turf, and we plan Central London visits very carefully because that's where parking and access are hardest. For portfolios spread across the capital, we group buildings by area so engineers spend their day servicing rather than sitting on the A40. If you've got several properties, an asset register with access notes makes every future visit easier. And if you're a landlord with HMOs scattered across boroughs, have a read of our HMO licensing post too.
