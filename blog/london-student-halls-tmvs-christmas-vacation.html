London student halls: using the Christmas vacation to get your TMVs serviced

27 November 2026By TMV Servicing London6 min read

Round about the middle of December, London's student blocks go quiet. Suitcases rattle off towards Euston and King's Cross, the communal kitchens finally get a rest, and a block that's been rammed since September is suddenly half empty for a few weeks. If you look after student accommodation, that's a window worth using.

Why the Christmas break is such a good time

Most purpose-built student blocks are full of en-suite rooms, and lots of those showers sit behind a thermostatic mixing valve or a thermostatic shower mixer. That's a lot of valves in one building. Servicing them during term means knocking on doors, working round lectures and 2pm lie-ins, and fitting in with whoever happens to be in.

Over the vacation it's a different story:

  • Rooms are empty. Our engineers can work floor by floor instead of hopping round the building chasing access.
  • Showers aren't in use. Isolating a valve for half an hour doesn't leave anyone shivering in a towel.
  • Problems get fixed before the rush. If a valve needs a new cartridge or a replacement, it's sorted before everyone's back and wanting a shower at the same time.

It's the same thinking as booking school valves over the summer, which we wrote about in servicing TMVs in London schools over the summer holidays. Shorter window, same idea.

It won't be completely empty

Worth saying, because it catches people out. Plenty of students stay over Christmas, especially those whose homes are a long way off. So a block can be half full, or a third full, and the people still there will be using the showers. That's fine, it just needs planning.

Get a list of which rooms will be occupied and which won't. Do the empty ones first and leave the occupied rooms for a set slot with proper notice. Check your tenancy terms for how much notice you need to give before entering a room, and let residents know what's happening and roughly how long the water will be off.

Tip: a short note under each door the week before saying "we're checking the shower valves, here's when" saves a lot of confused messages to the front desk.

Empty rooms and standing water

Here's the flip side. A room that's empty for three or four weeks has a shower that's not being used. Water sitting in the pipework, and in the warm mixed section after a TMV in particular, is exactly what your Legionella risk assessment worries about.

HSG274 Part 2 expects little-used outlets to be flushed at least weekly, and ACOP L8 puts the duty on whoever's in control of the building to manage that. So the vacation needs a flushing plan as well as a service plan. The two fit together nicely. When we service a valve, the outlet gets a thorough run as part of the job, and that can go on your flushing record for that week. It doesn't replace the regime for the rest of the break, though.

Before students come back in January, every outlet in an empty room should be flushed, following your written scheme. Hot water first, then cold, then a quick temperature check at the TMV outlets. If the building's communal plant was turned down over the break to save energy, make sure it's back to temperature well before the first arrivals.

London blocks bring London problems

Student accommodation in London tends to be in busy spots. Stratford and the Olympic Park in Newham, Whitechapel and Mile End in Tower Hamlets, King's Cross in Camden, Elephant and Castle in Southwark, and around Wembley in Brent. Those are great places to live and not always great places to park a van full of kit.

So we'll want to know about the loading bay, whether there's a booking system for the goods lift, how keys and fobs are signed out, and who's on site over Christmas. Our post on planning TMV servicing around London CPZs and access goes into this in more detail. The short version: sort access early and the visit goes smoothly.

The other London thing is hard water. Showers that run hard all term in a hard-water area build up scale in strainers and on thermostatic elements, so a valve that's had a busy autumn often benefits from a proper clean and descale.

What a vacation service visit looks like

Imagine a purpose-built block in East London with a few hundred en-suite rooms and a mix of shared flats. Here's roughly how we'd plan it:

  1. Start with the register. Which rooms have TMVs, what make and type, when they were last done. If there isn't one, we'll build it as we go. Our post on building a TMV asset register explains why that matters.
  2. Work floor by floor. Empty rooms first, occupied rooms in their notified slots.
  3. Service and test each valve. Clean strainers and check valves, check the element, set to the right temperature for the outlet and do a fail-safe test by isolating the cold.
  4. Flag the accessible rooms. Accessible bathrooms are where a resident might be more at risk from hot water, so we make sure those valves are the right type and set properly.
  5. Record everything. Readings, set points, parts fitted, anything that needs a follow-up. You get the lot, ready for your compliance file and the university or operator's audit.

A few things to sort now

  • Book early. Everyone with a student block has the same idea, and the vacation is only a few weeks long.
  • Get an occupancy list from your accommodation team as soon as you can.
  • Check who's on site over Christmas and New Year to let us in.
  • Line up the flushing regime for empty rooms and the pre-arrival flush in January.

If your hot water's been playing up during a cold snap, have a read of checking your TMVs after a burst main or frozen pipe too.

The short version

The Christmas vacation is a short, handy gap to get your valves serviced while nobody needs the showers. Plan the access, keep the flushing going and have it all on record before the January return.
